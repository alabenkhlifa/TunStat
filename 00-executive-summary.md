# Executive summary

## The situation in July 2026

Tunisia entered the summer with an economy that was growing again but remained structurally fragile. Real GDP rose 2.6% year on year in the first quarter of 2026, while quarterly output fell 0.3%. The World Bank expects 2.5% growth for 2026. June inflation was 5.3%, but food prices increased 7.1%. The labor market showed the deeper problem: 15.0% unemployment, 37.5% youth unemployment and 24.2% graduate unemployment in the first quarter.

The external position also weakened. Imports grew faster than exports in the first half of 2026, expanding the trade deficit from TND 9.90bn to TND 12.57bn. The energy component alone reached TND 6.78bn. Through May, domestic primary energy resources declined 9% while demand increased 4%, lowering energy independence from 39% to 34%.

The July electricity cuts were the operational expression of that imbalance. During exceptional heat and high evening demand, STEG used localized, rotating cuts in several governorates to protect the network. It also denied rumors of a nationwide blackout. Available evidence supports a diagnosis of peak-hour system stress, not proof of a complete national generation collapse. The response must therefore address both immediate operating margins and structural weaknesses: gas dependence, insufficient flexible capacity and storage, delayed network investment, losses and collection problems, weak outage transparency, and STEG’s difficult financial position.

Public finances limit the room for a simple spending response. The Ministry of Finance’s provisional 2025 execution shows TND 58.47bn of expenditure excluding lending. Wages absorbed 39.8%, transfers and interventions 34.5%, interest 11.0%, and investment only 9.8%. Subsidies were TND 11.59bn, of which fuel represented 61.3%. Public debt was TND 141.67bn, or 82.1% of GDP, and debt service reached TND 24.44bn. The World Bank forecasts a 2026 fiscal deficit of 6.1% of GDP and debt of 83.3%.

There are also real assets to build on. Declared investment rose 39.3% in 2025 to TND 8.36bn, although declarations are intentions rather than completed capital formation. Recorded international investment reached TND 3.57bn. Tunisia has an unusually consequential project pipeline: the World Bank-backed TEREG electricity program, the 600 MW ELMED interconnector, renewable auctions with storage, Bizerte bridge, a new water-security program, the Zarat desalination expansion, road upgrades, and phosphate-rail rehabilitation. The main execution risk is not a lack of project ideas. It is the gap between announcement, financing, procurement, construction and operation.

## What should happen first

### First 100 days

1. **Create a summer electricity reliability command.** STEG, the Energy Ministry, Interior, Water Ministry, hospitals, telecom operators and large industrial users should operate from one daily system plan. Publish available capacity, forecast peak demand, reserve margin, imports, major faults and any planned load-shedding windows.
2. **Protect essential loads.** Verify backup supply for hospitals, water pumping, wastewater, telecom, cold chains and transport control. Publish an exemption map and a procedure for reporting failures.
3. **Buy operating margin, not headlines.** Fast-track critical spares, transformer maintenance, contracted demand response and temporary flexibility where technically justified. Do not sign opaque emergency capacity contracts without price and availability disclosure.
4. **Start a public arrears and receivables settlement.** Identify amounts owed among the state, municipalities, public enterprises and STEG; net undisputed claims; place contested amounts in a time-bound resolution process. Pair collection with social protection and installment plans.
5. **Establish a fiscal cash and debt committee.** Publish a 13-week cash forecast, monthly arrears, guarantees, debt maturities and project disbursement. No new guarantee should be issued without a disclosed fiscal-risk assessment.
6. **Protect purchasing power precisely.** Preserve lifeline energy use and basic food support during the transition, but begin registering beneficiaries and paying targeted transfers before broad price support is reduced.
7. **Unblock ready investments.** Create a public permit and connection queue with statutory decision dates. For each stalled project, publish the missing decision, responsible institution and deadline.

### First 18 months

- Instrument the electricity system: outage management, smart metering for high-value feeders, SCADA and energy-loss measurement.
- Contract industrial and commercial demand response for peak hours.
- Commission the renewable projects already awarded and complete bankable tenders for solar, wind and battery storage.
- Move low-income support from generalized energy subsidies to a verified social registry and cash transfer, with a lifeline tariff.
- Review every capital project using readiness, economic return, resilience, regional impact and operating-cost criteria.
- Put the largest public enterprises under audited performance contracts covering service, cost, collections, debt, procurement and government compensation.
- Adopt a credible foreign-exchange reform package, with immediate operating facilities for legitimate exporters, startups and imported production inputs.
- Reduce approval uncertainty through silence-is-consent only where safety is not at stake, a single digital file, published reasons for refusal and an independent appeal.
- Protect public investment and maintenance while controlling recurrent expenditure through attrition, redeployment, payroll controls and digital service redesign rather than abrupt layoffs.

## Medium-term program: 18 months to five years

### Electricity and energy

The official TEREG program provides a usable backbone: mobilize private investment, add 2.8 GW of solar and wind by 2028, reduce supply costs, improve STEG cost recovery and reduce subsidies. Tunisia should add four disciplines:

- an independent electricity regulator with published tariff and grid-connection methods;
- a transparent connection queue and least-cost transmission plan;
- standardized, competitively awarded power-purchase contracts with disclosed contingent liabilities; and
- explicit reliability procurement for storage, flexible generation and demand response.

Tariff reform should be gradual. A lifeline block and targeted cash transfer must be operational before increases affect vulnerable households. Large users should move first toward time-of-use and cost-reflective pricing. ELMED should strengthen domestic reliability and regional trade; exports should not displace protected domestic demand during scarcity.

### Public finance and debt

Tunisia needs a medium-term fiscal framework that is binding enough to change annual decisions. The proposed operational anchors are:

- stabilize debt below 84% of GDP during the initial investment and reform phase;
- reduce the overall deficit below 4% by 2028 and toward 3% by 2030, subject to growth and social conditions;
- publish an annual fiscal-risk statement covering guarantees, public enterprises, PPPs, arrears and central-bank financing;
- cap new guarantees and charge a risk fee unless Parliament explicitly authorizes a social-policy exception;
- protect maintenance and high-return capital spending through a minimum budget share; and
- use any subsidy savings first for targeted transfers, debt reduction and energy/water investment.

Repeated direct central-bank financing should not become a substitute for fiscal adjustment. The TND 11bn zero-interest facility authorized in the 2026 Finance Law is exceptional. Any use should be disclosed monthly, limited by an exit schedule, and prohibited for new permanent recurrent commitments.

### Investment, finance and jobs

Government borrowing from domestic banks crowds out firms even when headline credit grows. Concessional project finance, longer debt maturities, domestic non-bank savings instruments and credible fiscal consolidation can release bank balance sheets. That should be paired with partial guarantees and first-loss facilities for viable SMEs, not directed lending without underwriting.

Job policy should connect training to actual purchase orders and investment projects. Apprenticeship contracts, employer-led curricula, women’s transport and childcare, and regional supplier programs around energy, water and transport projects will produce more durable employment than temporary public recruitment.

The 2025 labor-law changes should retain their protection against abusive outsourcing and perpetual fixed-term work, while implementing guidance should distinguish genuine specialized, seasonal and project work. A fast labor-dispute track would reduce uncertainty for both workers and employers.

## Long-term program: five to fifteen years

Tunisia’s durable strategy should have five aims:

1. **Reliable, lower-cost energy:** a diverse power mix, storage, efficient buildings and industry, stronger interconnection, and much lower technical and commercial losses.
2. **Water security:** desalination powered increasingly by renewables, leakage reduction, wastewater reuse, aquifer protection and crop choices aligned with water availability.
3. **Export complexity:** move from low-margin assembly toward engineering, electronics, automotive and aerospace components, pharmaceuticals, business services, software, agrifood brands and low-carbon industrial inputs.
4. **Human capital:** foundational learning, applied technical education, research-industry links and a credible route for diaspora skills and capital to return or circulate.
5. **Capable institutions:** stable rules, independent regulators and courts, open contracting, professional public-enterprise boards, and local governments able to plan and maintain infrastructure.

## The central legal changes

The dossier recommends targeted amendments or new laws rather than a complete legal reset:

- amend the Organic Budget Law’s implementation rules to require a fiscal-risk statement, tax-expenditure report, project pipeline and measurable performance reporting;
- create an independent electricity regulator and modern grid code under the renewable-electricity framework;
- consolidate public-enterprise governance in a new law with a state ownership policy, professional boards, audited accounts and explicit compensation for public-service obligations;
- amend procurement rules to require open contract data, beneficial ownership, change-order publication and performance information;
- refine PPP rules so affordability and contingent liabilities are independently reviewed before signature;
- adopt the pending foreign-exchange code with risk-based liberalization and clear safeguards;
- issue labor-law implementing rules that protect employees without making legitimate specialist and project work legally uncertain;
- strengthen the Competition Council’s independence, data access and enforcement capacity; and
- establish quantitative, transparent limits and an exit mechanism for monetary financing of the budget.

## How success should be judged

The program should not be evaluated by laws passed or loans signed. It should be judged by outcomes:

- outage minutes and unserved energy;
- electricity system reserve margin, losses, collections and cost recovery;
- debt, deficit, arrears and guarantee exposure;
- capital-budget execution and project delay;
- time to start a business operation, obtain a permit and connect to the grid;
- private credit, realized investment and export complexity;
- employment, female participation and youth placement after training;
- water losses, reuse and service continuity; and
- household poverty and energy burden after subsidy reform.

The final safeguard is simple: reforms that reduce a headline deficit while damaging reliability, nutrition, school attendance or productive investment are not successful reforms. Sequencing and compensation are part of the economics, not an afterthought.
